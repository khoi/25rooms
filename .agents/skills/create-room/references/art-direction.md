# Art direction beyond the benchmark

The aim is a miniature world that invites prolonged looking. The original rooms establish the drawing language and a minimum level of craft. Greater density alone does not exceed them.

## Design the image before its inventory

Write one sentence describing the room's striking visual idea and one describing the human moment. Sketch the large dark, middle, and light masses. The principal object and architecture should be identifiable with the little accessories hidden.

Choose a spatial design specific to the activity: a wraparound work bay, narrow multilevel dwelling, sweeping rack, curved boat hull, sloping glasshouse, layered stair, clustered courtyard, or another researched form. Avoid repeatedly placing a rectangular table in the center of a square room with a cabinet on each wall.

Give the foreground an intentional role. It can introduce the activity through an open case, exposed construction, stacked work, growing edge, or threshold. Use overlaps to lead through the middle to a deep rear boundary. Keep a believable route and a visible pocket around the important hands.

## Build a centerpiece worth studying

Replace plain fixture blocks with the object's actual construction. Use curves, open frames, bracing, recessed openings, angled surfaces, exposed mechanisms, shaped legs, hardware, imperfect contents, and visibly different materials where they belong. An intricate object has interacting parts, not just lines applied to a box.

Examples: a screen-printing table has a deep timber frame, inset mesh, clamps, hinge bearings, a supported lifting arm, registration stops, layered stock, and a wet work area. A kitchen has fitted cabinetry, recessed appliances, a sink with basin depth, plate storage, layered preparation surfaces, and tools arranged around an unfinished meal. A garden combines growth stages, branching habits, support systems, soil, decaying matter, and the apparatus of care.

The most important object deserves the greatest drawing effort. Do not spend the detail budget on distant fasteners while the center remains a blank rectangle.

## Make materials and light do visible work

Separate broad planes before adding texture. Establish a light source, cast shadows, contact shadows, interior darkness, and selective highlights. Place the clearest value contrast around the human action. A room-wide uniform halftone is not lighting.

Use different visual behavior for different materials: metal has thin edges, seams, reflections, and dark recesses; timber has thickness, joints, end grain, and softened wear; fabric has tension, folds, hems, and uneven drape; glass has a reveal, a view beyond it, and restrained reflection; plants have branching and varied leaf silhouettes. Marks follow the object and its perspective.

Choose two or three room-specific material signatures that are legible at normal size. Do not give every object the same outline weight, box proportions, and pattern.

## Tell a small story through relationships

Make at least three relationships legible without a caption: something in progress and its tool; a habit and its repeated trace; an imperfection and its repair; or two people responding to one another. A loose screw alone is clutter. A missing fastening, the screw in a tray, and a tool beside the opened panel tell a story.

Use asymmetry with cause. Leave a drawer partly open, a finished item beside an unfinished one, a replaced component in another color, or a cloth caught under a weight. Ensure each detail is visible at the supported close view.

Animation should change the scene's meaning or reveal construction. A useful loop can expose a mechanism, lift a lid, align a material, or reveal an exchange. Give actions anticipation, contact, a pause, and a supported return. Still poses must retain the story.

## Replace weak drawing rather than burying it

When revising an existing room, name the architecture or focal object being replaced. Remove superseded faces and accessories so the new construction has clear ownership and depth order. Place foundations and shadows first, rear structures second, furniture and contents next, and necessary foreground occlusion last. Preserve IDs and navigation unless the user requests otherwise.

Share physical geometry where it improves consistency, but keep compositions and signature forms in individual room modules. A helper must not decide the layout, inventory, story, and lighting for a whole collection.

## Judge a rendered comparison

Review the new room beside its previous version and a relevant original at matching sizes. Assess these six qualities separately:

| Quality | Evidence in the image |
| --- | --- |
| Composition | A distinctive silhouette, deliberate large masses, and a clear route for the eye |
| Architecture | Thick boundaries, believable attachment, openings, levels, and useful occupied edges |
| Centerpiece | Specific construction, visible depth, varied shapes, and operational relationships |
| Materials and light | Distinguishable materials, contact, directional light, and controlled contrast |
| Human story | Readable gestures and several relationships that imply life beyond the still frame |
| Discovery | Different kinds of details distributed across depths and heights, visible close up |

Use written observations, not an aggregate score. To support a claim of exceeding an original, show a clear gain in at least two qualities, including composition, centerpiece construction, or human storytelling, without a major regression elsewhere. If that evidence is absent, describe the improvement accurately and continue the authorized work.

Do not infer quality from source-line counts, drawing-call counts, inventory totals, polished prose, or successful tests. A contact sheet establishes variety but cannot establish craftsmanship. Review the last rooms with the same care as the first. The word “wow” is a design ambition, not a claim the author can validate on the user's behalf.

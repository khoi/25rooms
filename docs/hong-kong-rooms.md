# Hong Kong, between water and windows

A collection of exactly 25 contemporary isometric scenes for 25rooms. The tour moves from the working harbour into dense neighbourhoods, then follows the rivers, wetlands, islands, and evening footways back to a single lit window. These are fictional households, businesses, and composite layouts. Sources establish local connections. They do not document the invented occupants, possessions, or floor plans.

Each scene occupies roughly 12 by 12 units, with open front edges and a clear route through its main activity. A tram, ferry, pier shelter, or bird hide uses equivalent boundaries. The large silhouette reads across the collection; hands and posture explain the activity at normal zoom. Around a quarter of the floor remains quiet. Water, distant buildings, and landmarks stay inside bounded background strips.

The shared palette remains paper, blue, teal, coral, and sun. Thin blue outlines, flat tonal planes, irregular printed texture, and selective hatching suggest tile, steel, timber, concrete, and cloth. Artwork contains no lettering. Labels belong in captions. Packages, cards, signs, and artwork use original abstract shapes. Clothing is contemporary and practical, with rehearsal garments limited to the opera room. Different seasons appear as separate visits across the year, rather than one literal day.

The animated action in each entry has a reversible inspection gesture or an explicit return through a door, screen, or storage area. Secondary motion stays quieter than the hands. Every room includes a still pose for reduced motion. A coral strap appears on a few unrelated possessions as a restrained color connection; it does not imply one person makes the whole journey.

Low sun enters from the water at rear right, catching rope and knuckles. The thermos sits improbably straight on a wedge of folded cloth. This opening room introduces the harbour through maintenance and touch, with no fish auction or passenger spectacle.

## Tour order and final design review

The tour has five movements of five rooms: the city starts work; materials pass through skilled hands; movement becomes recreation; the outer edges slow the pace; and evening returns to the dense city. The order follows changes in attention and light rather than a feasible one-day route. The wetland room belongs to winter, the rain landing to summer, and the dragon preparation to its festival season.

Related scenes have different dominant geometry and human actions. Aberdeen uses a secured rope and curved bow; the harbour ferry uses a long passenger deck; Lamma uses a low parcel trolley. The rowing bay has high parallel equipment, while the kayak bay has a low rounded hull and rinse stream. Fabric is selected with a stretched tape, printing uses a broad two-handed practice stroke, and porcelain uses a supported plate and fine inspection. North Point's low produce tiers differ from Mong Kok's tall bucket arc. The three homes center on shared furniture, a raised water-edge work platform, and a solitary illuminated tank.

All signs, screens, packages, and diagrams stay free of lettering. Hands retain their props, moving objects meet supports before release, and repetitive studies use inspections or recoverable gestures. Still poses keep the key object beside the person who uses it. The artwork's visual review must inspect the full collection for repeated silhouettes, then check hands, foreground occlusion, and the first and last frames of each loop at room scale.

The research supports city and neighborhood associations, the milk-tea preparation stages, bamboo-theatre materials, the Guangcai painting tradition, opera's performance vocabulary, and the places' documented uses. Invented layouts, family stories, exact storage arrangements, abstract printed motifs, and reversible practice gestures are design proposals. Specialist construction knots, opera role choreography, and ritual preparation sequences are deliberately not claimed as technical reconstructions. A future documentary treatment would need focused maker interviews and measured references before adding those details.

Sources were opened and read on September 17, 2026. Some district pages describe long-lived places rather than current operating schedules; this collection makes no promises about opening hours, access, or business continuity.
